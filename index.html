Why we started

Pollution in Loudoun happens every day, and most of it is never measured.

Solving this will take longer than we have. We think it still starts the same way: pick a body of water, measure it honestly, write down what you found, and do it again next month.

Last year a 64-year-old Virginia man was wading in shallow brackish water near Virginia Beach with a small cut on his leg. The wound was exposed to Vibrio vulnificus, a bacterium that occurs naturally in warm, salty and brackish water, including near the Chesapeake Bay and the Potomac. He died. Most people have never heard of Vibrio, and cases like this are rare — but they are the sharp end of something ordinary. Urban runoff kills aquatic life, pushes fish past the point where they are safe to eat, and knocks the balance of a waterway out of order. It happens because dumping runoff is cheaper than treating it. Stretches of the Potomac are unsafe to swim in today, between the algal blooms, the runoff, and the bacteria that come with them.

Our own start was smaller. One of us, Sudhit, went fishing on rivers near the Potomac and came home with nothing — algae, plastic bags, bottles. He complained to a friend who had been to a different spot and seen exactly the same thing. So we gathered a few more people and started the Aqua Track Project. The more we read about the water in Loudoun County, the clearer it got: stable freshwater ecosystems here are the exception, not the rule. That became the whole focus.

Built

A probe, not an opinion

An ESP32 rig that reads the parameters we care about and condenses them into a two-sentence report on the water it just sampled. Bench-tested on tap water first, so we know what a clean baseline looks like.

In progress

Walk the reaches

Carry the probe to access points along the Potomac and its Loudoun tributaries, plus the neighborhood ponds nobody samples, and log every reading with its location.

Next

Publish the map

Put every reading on a public map under one color-coded index, so a neighbor can see how the water near them is doing without reading a technical report.

Long run

Be the boring part

A county with more stable freshwater than it has now, and a record consistent enough that whoever comes after us can pick it up and keep going.

Project log

Where the work actually stands.

Updated as things move. Anything filed under Next or Later is planned, not finished — we would rather say so than pad the list.

  1. Now

    • Probe v1 is reading

      The ESP32 rig is assembled and bench-tested on tap water. It takes its parameter readings and writes a two-sentence summary of the sample.

    • Entered Step Up Loudoun Youth

      The competition is our starting line, not the finish. It gets the project in front of the county so the map has an audience when it goes up.

    • Field sampling paused for winter

      Cold weather is keeping us off the water, which is why the map is still empty. It fills in as soon as we are back out.

  2. Next

    • First reaches sampled

      Goose Creek, Broad Run and Difficult Run, plus the neighborhood ponds around Ashburn and Leesburg — the small water that never gets a data point.

    • Readings go on the map

      Each sampled reach gets its grade and the date it was taken, under the same color-coded index shown above.

    • Field updates on Instagram

      Short posts from each sampling trip, so following along does not require checking the site.

  3. Later

    • A county-wide picture

      Enough repeat readings across Loudoun that the map shows change over time, not just one afternoon's snapshot.

    • Something worth handing off

      A record and a method consistent enough that the next group of students can pick it up without starting over.

Take action

Six people cannot fix a county's water.

That is not modesty, it is arithmetic. What actually moves the number is what happens on the ground upstream of every reach on that chart — driveways, lawns, storm drains. Most of it is small and most of it is free.

Keep it out of the storm drain

A storm drain is not a sink. Whatever goes down it — paint, oil, soapy car-wash water, grass clippings — arrives in a creek untreated, usually within the hour.

Sweep, don't hose

Fertilizer and grass clippings on a driveway wash straight into the nearest waterway and feed the algal blooms that pull the oxygen out. Sweep them back onto the grass instead.

Go lighter on the salt

Road and driveway salt spikes conductivity in winter and does not break down. A thin, deliberate spread clears ice about as well as a thick one.

Pick up after your dog

Pet waste left near a pond is a direct bacterial load on the water. It is the single easiest input on this list to remove.

The other thing that helps is attention. Follow the sampling, check the map when it fills in, and tell someone upstream of you about it.
